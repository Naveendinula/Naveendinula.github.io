Multi-Objective Optimization of Building Envelope Using Wallacei

Exploring the trade-offs between thermal comfort, energy use, and daylight through evolutionary algorithms.

Optimization Workflow

Tools: Rhino + Grasshopper + Ladybug Tools 1.8 + Honeybee + Wallacei X
EPW/STAT Source: Seattle-Tacoma International Airport TMYx
Model Geometry: Two adjacent shoebox zones, each 450 m³ (15m W × 10m L × 3m H)
Analysis Scope: 5,000 design iterations across 100 generations optimizing façade parameters for energy, comfort, and daylight.

1. Introduction

This project investigates how parametric optimization can enhance the environmental performance of building envelopes in marine climates. Using the Wallacei plugin for Grasshopper, a multi-objective evolutionary algorithm (MOEA) was applied to explore 5,000 façade design variations simulated through Honeybee-Energy (EnergyPlus).

The optimization targeted three competing objectives: minimizing heating load, cooling load, and overheating risk (%). It investigates how façade parameters such as window-to-wall ratio, shading depth, and orientation influence thermal comfort and energy performance. The study aims to identify balanced design configurations that enhance low-energy building envelopes while maintaining occupant comfort.

3. Objective Space Analysis

The Objective Space in Wallacei visualizes the performance of all 5,000 simulated design options across the three optimization goals: cooling load, heating load, and overheating risk (%). Each point represents a unique façade configuration, collectively forming a Pareto front that reveals the trade-offs between competing objectives.

This surface illustrates the trade-offs between objectives:

  • Heating vs. Cooling: When heating demand is minimized through higher solar gains and larger south-facing glazing, cooling demand tends to increase due to greater solar exposure.
  • Heating vs. Overheating: Designs that lower heating demand often face higher overheating risk for the same reason—greater solar gain that benefits winter comfort can cause excessive summer heat.
  • Cooling vs. Overheating: Solutions that minimize cooling load also tend to maintain low overheating percentages, but this comes with higher heating requirements caused by reduced passive solar gains.

This trade-off is characteristic of the marine climate in the Pacific Northwest, where mild summers and extended heating seasons create tension between minimizing winter heat loss and avoiding summer overheating. The Pareto front did not yield a single optimal configuration but rather a continuum of balanced solutions. From these, the most effective designs achieved moderate heating loads with controlled solar exposure, demonstrating that façade balance is more impactful than simply maximizing or minimizing a single objective.

4. Standard Deviation Analysis

The Standard Deviation (SD) graphs and trend lines visualize how solution diversity changed over generations.

High deviation indicates exploration (broad variation between solutions), while low deviation shows convergence (similar, high-performing designs).

Tracking SD across objectives helps reveal when the algorithm stabilizes — signaling a balanced optimization process.

Cooling Load (FO1)

  • Early generations show high variation (red curves), reflecting broad exploration of cooling strategies.
  • As evolution progresses, curves tighten into blue clusters, and the SD trend line flattens — indicating stabilization and convergence toward consistent, efficient cooling solutions.
  • Cooling standard deviation appears to stabilize between 0.62 and 0.63 after generation 90.

Heating Load (FO2)

  • Initial generations show wider performance spread, which gradually narrows as the algorithm learns optimal heating strategies.
  • The SD trend rises and then levels off, showing that the population converged around stable heating performance with reduced variability.
  • Heating standard deviation appears to stabilize between 2.9 and 3.0 after generation 90.

Overheating Percentage (FO3)

  • High initial deviation highlights early exploration of comfort performance.
  • Over time, curves contract, and the SD trend stabilizes — marking reduced performance volatility and convergence toward well-performing comfort zones.
  • Heating standard deviation appears to stabilize between 9.0 and 9.2 after generation 90.

Summary

Across all three objectives, Wallacei's SD analysis shows a smooth transition from exploration → convergence, confirming that the evolutionary process effectively balanced diversity and optimization stability.

5. Clustering & Solution Grouping

We applied K-means clustering to the Pareto set to reveal families of solutions with similar performance across Cooling, Heating, and Overheating%. Three distinct clusters emerged:

The clustering revealed three distinct performance families, each representing a different design trade-off strategy:

  • Cluster 1: Solar-open designs: Lower heating but higher cooling and overheating%. These solutions generally feature higher WWR and/or higher SHGC with modest shading, which helps winter demand but elevates summer peaks.
  • Cluster 2: Balanced designs: Comparable levels across all three objectives, typically landing near the ≤10% overheating constraint. Mid-range WWR, SHGC, and shading geometry characterize this group.
  • Cluster 3: Solar-protected designs: Lower cooling and overheating% at the expense of higher heating. These solutions use lower WWR or SHGC and deeper/steeper shading to control summer gains.

12. Conclusion

This project was both a technical study and a learning exercise in using Wallacei for multi-objective building performance optimization. By linking parametric façade design with energy simulation, it demonstrated how evolutionary algorithms can reveal trade-offs between energy demand and thermal comfort. The results identified balanced solutions that reduced cooling load and overheating risk, while highlighting their inverse relationship with heating demand in a Pacific Northwest climate.

Beyond the numerical outcomes, the process provided valuable insight into how multi-objective algorithms evolve, stabilize, and converge across conflicting goals. It also improved understanding of how to interpret Pareto fronts, cluster results, and translate data into design guidance.

Some methodological limitations were identified. The use of separate façade WWRs along with building rotation created redundancy, making it difficult to isolate orientation effects. Future improvements could include simplifying the gene structure, using combined façade ratios, and adding daylight or comfort metrics to the objectives.

Overall, the project achieved its aim of building technical knowledge in parametric optimization while producing meaningful energy performance insights. It provides a strong foundation for future studies that explore adaptive envelopes, refined HVAC strategies, and real-world case applications.
